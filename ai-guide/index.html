AI for propertyprofessionals

A practical guide to getting started with AI at work.

You can use AI at work. You just don't quite know where to start, or you've tried it a few times and you're still getting nowhere.

See if any of this sounds familiar:

  • You've been told to use AI, then left to work out what that actually means
  • You rebuild the same report from scratch every week, and it still lands last minute
  • You asked for something, got a confident answer back, and couldn't tell whether to trust it
  • You rewrite so much of the output that doing it yourself would have been quicker
  • You're not sure which parts of your job should stay yours

If so, the problem isn't the tool. None of that gets solved by switching to a different one.

This guide is for property professionals who want to use AI more confidently, cut the time repetitive work takes, and make informed decisions about when and how to use it.

Build confidence. Create capacity. Make more room for growth.

Where should you start? Pick whichever is closest to how you work.

01 Mindset

AI is a skill, not just a tool

Knowing how to open an AI tool is very different from knowing how to use it well.

The opportunity is learning to work with it in a way that helps you think more clearly, communicate more effectively, and get through everyday tasks with greater confidence. In property, that might mean:

  • Helping the marketing team build a design system, so everything the company produces looks like it came from the same place
  • Helping the aftercare team triage enquiries
  • Helping a project manager turn meeting notes into actions
  • Helping a design team structure a brief

You do not need to become a technical expert. You need to become a more confident and thoughtful user. That takes more than typing a question into a chat box.

02 Fundamentals

Prompting: start with the end in mind

Before you open an AI tool, take a moment to think about the result you actually want.

  • Are you writing a polished email, and who is receiving it?
  • Trying to understand a spreadsheet before an important board meeting, with no time to spare?
  • Organising feedback for the team into a practical format?
  • Pulling a project update together from several sources, covering everything from what's progressing well to where the budget has blown?
  • Working out how to approach a task that just feels complicated?

The clearer you are about the outcome, the easier it becomes to explain what you need. And, crucially, what you don't need.

The three questions I'd ask

01

What does a good result look like?

Be specific about what you want to achieve, the format you want it in, and who the result is for.

02

What does the AI need to know?

Give it the background, constraints, context and source material. Don't assume it understands your project, your team's terminology, or what you mean by a phrase you use every day.

03

What else does it need from you?

Before asking it to do anything complicated, invite it to spot what's missing and ask you questions.

Try this at the end of any prompt

      
If you only take one line from this guide, take this one. It stops the AI charging ahead on a task it has misunderstood.

      

        
The clarify-first line

        
Before you start, do you understand the task? If you're unsure, ask me any questions you need answered to understand the task, the context and the outcome I want. Do not begin until you have clarified anything important that is missing. If you think something is missing that you need, ask me about that too. If you already have enough information, tell me what assumptions you are making before you proceed.

      

      
This helps you find the gaps before the work begins. For simple tasks you may not need a lengthy exchange, so use your judgement about which questions genuinely matter.


      
You don't always have to type

      
Sometimes speaking is a much easier way to explain a complicated task than trying to construct the perfect written prompt. You can dictate using your device's microphone, or an approved speech-to-text tool:

      
    
        
  • Wispr Flow

Speaking naturally helps you get your thoughts out without worrying about every sentence.

      
At work: always consider the sensitivity of what you're saying out loud and where that information is being processed. Don't put confidential business or personal information through an unapproved transcription service.

      
And if you are typing but don't have time to go into detail, ask the AI to ask you follow-up questions instead. The questions do the work the long prompt would have done.


      
Ask AI how to use AI

      
You don't need to know the perfect workflow before you begin.

      

        
Starter prompt

        
I need to complete [task] for my role at a [type of business]. The outcome I'm trying to achieve is [outcome]. Can you help me understand how I could use AI to assist with this task, and what other tools might help?

      

      
Think of prompting as a conversation. You can refine your request, challenge the response, and ask the tool to explain its reasoning or its assumptions.

      
The aim is not to write the longest prompt. It's to give the AI enough of the right information to get you the result you need.

    

  


  
  

    
03 Set-up

    

      
Stop starting from scratch every time

      
If you build the same report every fortnight, you shouldn't be rebuilding the instructions every fortnight too.

      
A lot of work looks like this: the same report, weekly or fortnightly, pulling information out of various sources and typing it into the same shape. Every time, from nothing.

      
Most AI tools now offer ways to save repetitive instructions. In Claude, for example:

      
    
        
  • Skills: a saved set of instructions that produces the same type of document or response every time you feed it new information.
    • 
        
  • Projects: a space where you can store context and keep one topic's conversations from getting muddled up with everything else.
    • 
        
  • Settings: I know. But a little context about you goes a long way: how you speak, what your role is, which company you sit in.
    • 
      

      
Other platforms have their own version. ChatGPT has custom GPTs you can configure for a particular purpose. Most tools have something.

      
Personalisation can include your role, your preferred writing style, your usual output format, and the questions you want the assistant to ask before it begins a task.

      
Personalisation isn't about having a long conversation with an AI assistant. It's about taking those few hours back on your next report.

And bring it closer to the work you already do

      
You don't have to move every task into a separate chat window. Depending on your set-up, AI tools can connect directly to your email, your Office files, your calendar, your design software and your file storage. In Claude these are called connectors; in ChatGPT you'll see them described as apps or plugins.

      
Check first: availability depends on the product, the version, the subscription, the configuration and your organisation's permissions. Don't assume a feature is available to you simply because you've seen it demonstrated somewhere else.

    

  


  
  

    
04 Workflows

    

      
Practical workflows for property teams

      
Four places AI earns its keep in a property business. Open the one closest to your role.


      


        

          
          

            
A cohesive brand across everyone in the business is hard at the best of times. AI makes it harder, because different teams are producing content at speed without the same oversight.

            
So use AI to hold the brand rather than dilute it:

            
    
              
  • Build a design system you can share across the company, so every output follows the same colours, brand perspective and voice.
    • 
              
  • Create a saved skill all teams can use, so client-facing documents sound on brand without marketing having to rewrite them.
    • 
              
  • Set up a posting assistant that helps colleagues write social posts in the brand's voice, so promoting the business doesn't fall to one person.
    • 
            

            

              
Try this

              
I want to create a shared brand guide that colleagues across the business can use when they write with AI, so everything we produce sounds like us.

I'll paste examples of our best client-facing copy below. From those, extract: our tone of voice rules, words and phrases we use, words and phrases we avoid, and our sentence and formatting habits. Then write it up as a set of instructions I can save and share.

Ask me anything you need first. Don't invent rules the examples don't support.

Examples:
[paste 3-5 pieces of your own copy]

            

            
Check before using: verify every factual claim, and make sure the final copy reflects the approved brand voice and your marketing requirements.

          

        


        

          
          

            
If you're a developer with several sites on the go, clear communication and effective triage are essential to keeping service levels up. They're also tough to maintain when everything lands in one inbox.

            
So don't stop at drafting an empathetic reply. Go further:

            
    
              
  • Use AI to review the out-of-date guides you've never had time to update, and flag where residents are being given the wrong information.
    • 
              
  • Triage the shared inbox: what's urgent, what isn't yours, and what can wait until tomorrow.
    • 
              
  • Set up scheduled summaries so the team starts the day knowing what actually needs them.
    • 
            

            

              
Try this

              
Help me triage this aftercare inbox. Sort the enquiries into four groups: urgent and mine, needs a reply today, can wait until tomorrow, and should go to another team.

For each one, give me a one-line reason and the name of the site it relates to. Use only what's in the messages. Don't guess at severity or make commitments on our behalf.

Flag anything that reads like a health and safety issue or a formal complaint at the top, separately.

            

            
Check before using: confirm the facts, the tone and any commitments made. Protect customer information and follow your organisation's approved processes.

          

        


        

          
          

            
Project managers constantly need to organise large amounts of information, capture decisions, and pull out the actions and the unanswered questions. AI is good at turning messy notes into a usable structure.

            

              
Try this

              
Before you start, tell me if anything important is missing.

Then organise these meeting notes into decisions, actions, risks and open questions. Only include owners and deadlines that are explicitly stated in the notes. Do not invent missing information. If an action has no owner, list it under open questions instead.

Notes:
[paste your notes]

            

            
Check before using: confirm that decisions, owners, dates and actions are accurate before you circulate the summary.

          

        


        

          
          

            
Design teams can use AI to organise requirements, structure feedback, prepare draft trackers, and make long documents easier to navigate. What it should not do is produce specifications.

            

              
Try this

              
Help me structure these agreed requirements into a design brief. Ask me about missing information first.

Separate confirmed requirements, assumptions, and unanswered questions into three clear sections. Do not create technical specifications or compliance claims that I have not supplied.

Requirements:
[paste your requirements]

            

            
Check before using: have the appropriate design or technical professional verify specifications, regulatory requirements and technical details.

          

        


      


      
One final thought on workflows

      
Don't start by trying to automate an entire department. Pick one small, repetitive task that takes up more of your week than it should, test whether AI can help, and learn from the result.

      
The best starting point is usually the task you understand well enough to judge whether the output is any good.

    

  


  
  

    
05 Responsibility

    

      
Responsible AI is a team responsibility

      
Experimenting on your own is easy. Using AI in a business is more complicated, because the information usually isn't yours.

      
It may belong to customers, colleagues, consultants or the organisation itself. A good starting point is to talk to the people and teams you work with: find out which tools are approved, what the company policy says, and which types of information can and cannot be used.

      
Treat other people's information with the same respect you'd want them to show yours.


      
When should you do the work yourself, and when should AI help?

      
This is one of the most important questions to ask, and it has no single answer.

      
Three things are true at once:

      
    
        
  • If you use AI to think through every problem before you've attempted to understand it yourself, you miss the chance to build your own skills
    • 
        
  • If you hand over work and don't understand the output, you won't notice when something is wrong
    • 
        
  • But refusing to use AI for anything repetitive means spending your time on work that could be done better another way
    • 
      

      
The answer isn't to automate everything or avoid AI altogether. It's to make a deliberate choice. Here's my process:

      

        

          
Think about it

          
When the task builds a skill you need, helps you understand a problem, or calls for professional judgement, develop that yourself first.

        

        

          
Collaborate with it

          
When you want to explore options, challenge your own thinking, organise information, or improve a first draft.

        

        

          
Delegate the suitable part

          
Once you understand the task well enough to judge the output, ask what part of it can reasonably be handed over.

        

        

          
Keep a person responsible

          
No outcome is free of professional expertise, authorisation, sensitive judgement or accountability. Don't sit back and press send without reading what's going out.

        

      

      
As AI becomes more capable, more of us will be managing AI-assisted work rather than doing all of it. That means understanding the objective, giving good instructions, checking the work, and knowing when to step in.

    

  


  
  

    
06 Habit

    

      
It starts with you

      
You don't need to transform your working day in a week. You need the habit of experimenting thoughtfully and iterating with intention.

      
Think of AI as a new colleague. It will make mistakes. It might even overwork. But over time it's about finding the sweet spot: letting it get on with work because you've given it the right direction, so you can focus on something new. Or on that thing you keep saying you'll do and never have time for.
